Coal-to-gas repowering: gas turbines and HRSG for an existing steam turbine

How heat recovery repowering turns a coal unit into a combined cycle plant, when it pays and which gas turbines fit.

What is coal-to-gas heat recovery repowering? Heat recovery repowering replaces the coal boiler of an existing power plant with one or more gas turbines and a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG); the gas turbine exhaust raises steam for the plant's existing steam turbine, so the coal unit becomes a combined cycle plant. It suits plants whose steam turbine, condenser, cooling system and grid connection still have decades of life left. LKS 360-SCHREINER LLC lists gas turbines and gas turbine trains with HRSG for such projects on behalf of their owners.

What is heat recovery repowering?

In heat recovery repowering the coal boiler of an existing power plant is taken out of service and replaced by one or more gas turbines with a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG). The gas turbines produce power directly, and their hot exhaust raises steam in the HRSG for the plant's existing steam turbine. The coal unit becomes a combined cycle plant that keeps its steam turbine, condenser, cooling system, switchyard and grid connection.

When does it pay?

  • Condition of the steam turbine and balance of plant: the steam turbine, condenser, cooling system and transmission connection have to be good for several more decades. According to Burns & McDonnell (April 2020), keeping the existing steam turbine essentially eliminates a third of the cost of a brand-new build if the equipment is in good working condition.
  • Existing site: a coal plant already has interconnection, transmission, water and industrial land. According to POWER magazine (September 2026), a retiring generator's interconnection rights can often transfer to a replacement resource, usually within a window of about a year.
  • Proven approach: the same POWER article states that from 2011 to 2019 more than 100 coal plants were converted to or replaced by gas.

Reference: according to Siemens Energy, Cooperative Energy replaced an existing coal-fired unit at the R.D. Morrow, Sr. Generating Station in Mississippi (coal station built in 1978) with an SGT6-9000HL gas turbine that works together with the plant's existing steam turbine. The plant delivers 550 MW, and Siemens Energy states a carbon footprint reduced by 50 percent compared with the coal unit.

The main difficulty: matching gas turbine and steam turbine

The steam an HRSG can raise follows from the exhaust flow and exhaust temperature of the gas turbine. It has to fit the design steam flow, live steam pressure and temperature, and any reheat of the existing steam turbine. If the gas turbine is too small, the steam turbine runs at part load; if it is too large, steam has to be bypassed or the gas turbine runs below its rating. The usual tools are:

  • the size and number of gas turbines,
  • supplementary firing in the HRSG,
  • the pressure levels and a reheat section of the HRSG to suit the steam turbine,
  • the space available where the coal boiler stood.

The basis for sizing is the heat balance of the steam turbine and the exhaust data of the gas turbine at site conditions, checked by the buyer's engineer.

Grid frequency: 50 Hz or 60 Hz

The gas turbine generator has to match the grid. A plant on a 60 Hz grid, as in North America, needs a 60 Hz gas turbine generator set or one rated for both frequencies. A plant on a 50 Hz grid, as in Europe and large parts of Asia, needs a 50 Hz unit. A geared gas turbine can sometimes be adapted with a different gearbox; a large direct drive gas turbine runs at grid speed and stays on its frequency. See also used 60 Hz units for the Americas.

What a buyer should prepare

  • Heat balance and design data of the existing steam turbine: steam flow, live steam and reheat pressure and temperature, extraction points, exhaust pressure.
  • Condition reports of steam turbine, generator, condenser and cooling system.
  • Grid frequency, voltage and the export capacity of the existing connection.
  • Fuel gas supply, emission limits and the permit route for the site.
  • Space where the coal boiler stood, for gas turbines and HRSG.

Frequent questions

Can an existing coal plant steam turbine be driven by gas turbines?

Yes. In heat recovery repowering the coal boiler is replaced by one or more gas turbines and a heat recovery steam generator, which raises steam for the existing steam turbine. The steam turbine, condenser and cooling system have to be in good condition, and the HRSG steam data have to match the steam turbine.

How much does reusing the steam turbine save?

According to Burns & McDonnell (2020), keeping the existing steam turbine essentially eliminates a third of the cost of a brand-new build, provided the equipment is in good working condition. The actual saving depends on the condition of the steam turbine, the condenser, the cooling system and the grid connection.

Has heat recovery repowering of a coal unit been done?

Yes. According to Siemens Energy, Cooperative Energy replaced an existing coal-fired unit at the R.D. Morrow, Sr. Generating Station in Mississippi, built in 1978, with an SGT6-9000HL gas turbine working together with the plant's existing steam turbine; the plant delivers 550 MW.

What is the main technical difficulty?

Matching the gas turbine to the steam turbine. The steam an HRSG can raise follows from the exhaust flow and temperature of the gas turbine, and it has to fit the design steam flow, pressure and temperature of the existing steam turbine. The usual tools are the choice of gas turbine size and number, supplementary firing in the HRSG and the HRSG pressure levels.

Does the gas turbine have to match the grid frequency?

Yes. A plant on a 60 Hz grid, as in North America, needs a 60 Hz gas turbine generator set or one rated for both frequencies. A plant on a 50 Hz grid, as in Europe and large parts of Asia, needs a 50 Hz unit.
